## The water cycle analogy

Imagine a water pipe with a pump. The **pump** creates pressure, the **water** flows, a **narrow pipe** slows the flow down. In electrical engineering these three things are called:

| Water | Electrical engineering | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pressure | **Spannung** (voltage) | *U* | Volt (**V**) |
| Amount of water per second | **Strom** (current, Stromstärke) | *I* | Ampere (**A**) |
| Narrowness of the pipe | **Widerstand** (resistance) | *R* | Ohm (**Ω**) |

Spannung is the "driving force", Strom is what actually flows, Widerstand is what limits it. No Spannung, no Strom. And no closed circuit, no Strom either: Strom only flows when it can travel from the source through the load and back to the source. That is why every Steckdose (socket outlet) has at least two contacts: **Außenleiter L** (line conductor, the way there) and **Neutralleiter N** (neutral conductor, the way back).

## Spannung U – the driving force

The **Spannung** is the difference in electrical "pressure" between two points. In Germany, the grid supplies **230 V** between an Außenleiter and the Neutralleiter (Wechselspannung (AC voltage), 50 Hz). Between two Außenleiter it is **400 V** – more on that on the page [Netz und Leiterfarben](../netz-und-leiterfarben/).

Remember: Spannung exists **between** two points. You measure it **in parallel** to the component, i.e. with the meter connected between the two terminals.

Typical values: AA battery 1.5 V · car battery 12 V · Steckdose 230 V · cooker/Drehstrom 400 V · medium-voltage grid 10–30 kV.

## Strom I – what flows

The **Stromstärke** states how much charge flows through the conductor per second. 1 ampere is one coulomb per second – you do not have to know that by heart, but you should know the orders of magnitude:

| Load | Strom at 230 V |
|---|---|
| LED lamp 8 W | 0.035 A (35 mA) |
| Laptop power supply 65 W | 0.3 A |
| Television 100 W | 0.4 A |
| Vacuum cleaner 900 W | 3.9 A |
| Kettle 2,000 W | 8.7 A |
| Fan heater 2,000 W | 8.7 A |
| Socket circuit, LS B16 | max. 16 A |

You measure Strom **in series** – the meter is connected into the circuit, or you use a **Stromzange** (current clamp) around the conductor. **An ammeter connected in parallel across 230 V is a short circuit.**

**Danger to people:** As little as about **30 mA** through the body can be fatal (ventricular fibrillation). That is why **RCD/FI protective devices** in homes trip at a residual current of 30 mA – see [Schutzorgane](../schutzorgane/).

## Widerstand R – what slows things down

Every conductor and every load has a **Widerstand**. A long, thin wire has more Widerstand than a short, thick one. Copper conducts well, iron less well, plastic almost not at all (**Isolator** (insulator)).

For cables the rule is: Widerstand grows with **length** and decreases with **cross-section**. That is why a long Zuleitung (supply cable) gets a larger cross-section – otherwise Spannung is lost along the way (**Spannungsfall** (voltage drop)) and the cable heats up.

Rule-of-thumb values for copper: 1.5 mm² ≈ 12 Ω per km · 2.5 mm² ≈ 7.4 Ω per km · 10 mm² ≈ 1.8 Ω per km (per core).

## Ohmsches Gesetz (Ohm's law)

The three quantities are connected:

> **U = R · I** – Spannung = Widerstand times Strom

Rearranged: **I = U / R** and **R = U / I**. This is the most important formula of your Ausbildung. A triangle helps you remember it: U on top, R and I next to each other at the bottom. Cover the quantity you are looking for – what remains is the calculation.

**Example 1:** A fan heater has 26.5 Ω. At 230 V, the current is I = 230 V / 26.5 Ω ≈ **8.7 A**.

**Example 2:** An incandescent lamp carries 0.26 A at 230 V. Its Widerstand is R = 230 V / 0.26 A ≈ **885 Ω** (when hot).

**Example 3:** In a 12 V vehicle electrical system, a lamp carries 2 A. R = 12 V / 2 A = **6 Ω**.

## Leistung P – how much work per second

The **Leistung** (power) is Spannung times Strom:

> **P = U · I** – Leistung in watts (**W**), 1,000 W = 1 kW

Combined with the Ohmsches Gesetz: **P = U² / R** and **P = I² · R**.

**Example:** Kettle 2,000 W at 230 V: I = P / U = 2,000 W / 230 V ≈ **8.7 A**. Two kettles on one power strip: 17.4 A – the **LS B16 trips**. That is not a defect, that is protection.

With AC and motors, the **Leistungsfaktor cos φ** also comes into play (apparent power S in VA, active power P in W). For resistive loads like heaters and incandescent lamps, cos φ = 1 – that is enough for a start.

## Energie W – Leistung times time

The electricity meter does not count Leistung, but **Energie**:

> **W = P · t** – energy in kilowatt-hours (**kWh**)

**Example:** Fan heater 2 kW, 3 hours: W = 2 kW · 3 h = **6 kWh**. At about 35 ct/kWh that is €2.10. An 8 W LED lamp needs 750 hours for 6 kWh.

## The units and their prefixes

| Prefix | Factor | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **m** (milli) | 0.001 | 30 mA tripping current of an RCD |
| **k** (kilo) | 1,000 | 2 kW kettle, 10 kV medium voltage |
| **M** (mega) | 1,000,000 | 1 MΩ minimum insulation resistance |

Always calculate in base units (V, A, Ω, W) and convert only at the end. Most calculation mistakes at vocational school are unit mistakes.

## What you can do now

- Distinguish Spannung, Strom, Widerstand and Leistung and name their units.
- Calculate any missing quantity with U = R · I and P = U · I.
- Understand why an LS-Schalter (circuit breaker) trips when too many devices are connected and why 30 mA is dangerous for the body.
