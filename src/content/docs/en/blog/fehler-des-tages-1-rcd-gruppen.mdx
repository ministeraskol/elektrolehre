## The situation

A brand-new Unterverteilung (sub-distribution board), two RCD groups, everything neatly labelled. Erstprüfung (final inspection) passed – or so they thought.
Then the customer switches on the washing machine and **RCD 2 trips**. Sometimes both of them.
No short circuit, no residual current, no faulty appliance. And yet: it trips.

## What happened

An RCD compares the current flowing out through **L** with the current coming back through **N**.
If both are equal, everything is fine. If the difference exceeds the Bemessungsdifferenzstrom (rated residual operating current) of 30 mA, it trips.

The fault: the Außenleiter (line conductor) of the washing machine is connected to **RCD 1**, but its Neutralleiter (neutral conductor) was screwed to the **N terminal of RCD 2**.
As long as no current flows, nobody notices anything. As soon as the machine runs, current flows out through L via RCD 1 –
and returns through N via RCD 2. For RCD 1, the return current is "missing"; for RCD 2, current "arrives" that never flowed out through it.
Both see a difference. At least one of them trips.

## What it should look like

Every final circuit belongs **completely** to one RCD group: L through the LS-Schalter (miniature circuit breaker) of that group, **N to the N terminal
of the same group**. The N terminals of the two groups must never be connected.

Rule of thumb at the Verteiler (distribution board): **"L and N go for a walk together."** Where the Außenleiter comes from, that's where the Neutralleiter goes.

## How to find it in 30 seconds

1. Switch off all circuits of the tripping group.
2. Switch the loads back on one by one – the circuit that makes it trip is the suspect.
3. Trace the Neutralleiter of that circuit in the Verteiler: does it land on the N terminal of the **other** group? Found it.
